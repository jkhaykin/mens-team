Erotic Partner Curiosity

Helping men reconnect with the living pulse of their partner’s erotic energy.

This is not a process for “getting” sex. It’s a process for deepening presence, listening, and curiosity — the real sources of erotic connection.

This process was inspired by a post from Britt Forhman, founder of the Juicy Ladies Retreat, on how women’s desire changes over time. It can also be shared with a partner as a starting point for conversation.

Part 1: In Group

Step 1: Group Sharing — What We’ve Tried, What Worked, and What Didn’t

The group explores:

  • How each man has brought up libido, desire, or erotic change with his partner.
  • Moments where these conversations felt threatening or sensitive for her, and what contributed to that.
  • Times when approaching with curiosity (rather than an agenda) opened connection.
  • Do men believe their partner’s erotic desires have shifted over time? For example, from external expectations like the “male gaze” toward more internal motivations, such as exploring what kinds of touch feel good to her in her body.
  • Has longing for “how sex used to be” helped or hurt intimacy?

This step is purely about real stories, not prescribed answers, letting the wisdom arise from what men have lived and experienced.

Step 2: Group Reflection — How We Want to Approach Curiosity Going Forward

After hearing everyone’s experiences, the group reflects, going around to each man with questions like:

  • What feels like a gentle, non-threatening way to express curiosity to a partner?
  • How can I ask about her desires now, today, without implying anything is wrong or trying to get more sex in the future?
  • What are ways to invite her internal erotic voice (what feels good to her body now) without pressure?
  • How do I imagine practicing ongoing, organic questions like, “What kind of touch would feel good to you?” or “What would you like to explore together tonight?”
  • How might I integrate what my partner tells me into my own desires and turn-ons?

The group forms a collective understanding of how curiosity, openness, and letting go of old sexual patterns can support a new chapter of intimacy.

Part 2: The Practice — “What’s Erotic for You Now?”

Step 1: Setting the Frame — Erotic Curiosity as Reverence

Ask your partner: What does “erotic” mean to you now? How has that changed over time?

Then ask yourself:

  • How do I usually respond when erotic connection fades: with frustration, withdrawal, or curiosity?
  • What might it look like to treat desire as something sacred, alive, and evolving, not as a problem to fix?

Key reminder: Eros is dynamic. It changes with age, parenthood, hormones, trauma, creativity, and even seasons of life. The invitation is to learn what’s true now, not to reclaim what was once true.

Step 2: Asking “What’s Erotic for You Now?”

Depending on your relational situation, the practice looks slightly different.

A. If you have a monogamous partner

Invite a real conversation, not an interrogation.

“I’ve been thinking about how desire changes over time. I’d love to know: what feels erotic or enlivening for you these days?”

If she’s open, reflect back what you hear and ask curious questions:

  • “What helps you access that feeling?”
  • “What gets in the way?”
  • “What has changed for you over the years?”

If she says “nothing,” meet that with gentle curiosity, not pressure:

  • “How does it feel to be in that space right now?”
  • “Is it really nothing, or more like something that feels out of reach?”

Your role: witness, not fixer.

B. If you have multiple or non-monogamous partners

You can use the same question, tailored to each connection:

“What feels erotic for you with me right now? Has that changed recently?”

Each relationship may hold a different erotic truth. Your practice is to listen for uniqueness without comparison, and to honor each partner’s evolving experience as its own living ecology.

Reflection afterward:

  • How does each person’s answer shape your understanding of your own desire?
  • Are there themes or contrasts?
  • What does “attunement” mean when you hold multiple relational threads?

C. If you have a partner but no sexual intimacy

Your practice is to stay in connection without forcing reconnection.

“I know things have been quiet between us sexually. I’d like to understand what’s happening for you now — not to pressure you, but to know you better.”

If she doesn’t want to talk about sex directly, ask broader questions:

  • “What helps you feel alive lately?”
  • “Where does pleasure show up in your life right now?”
  • “What does closeness look like for you these days?”

Reflection afterward:

  • How can you stay erotically alive without demanding reciprocity?
  • What forms of intimacy (emotional, creative, sensual) still feel available?
  • What do you discover about your own patience, grief, or longing?

Step 3: Integration — Sharing and Reflection

Back in your men’s group, reflect on:

  • What did you learn about your partner(s), yourself, or your relationship to desire?
  • What surprised you?
  • Where did you feel defensive, inspired, or confused?
  • What happens when you approach eros as a mystery rather than a goal?

Listen without fixing. Protect confidentiality. Honor vulnerability as courage.

Step 4: Living the Practice

Erotic curiosity isn’t a one-time exercise — it’s a muscle. Keep asking:

  • “What feels good right now?”
  • “What kind of closeness or connection is alive between us today?”
  • “How has what turns me on changed?”
  • “How might I honor that change, in myself or others, with reverence rather than resistance?”

Eroticism thrives in safety, spaciousness, and surprise. Approach it like a garden: tending, not forcing.

Background: A Woman’s Perspective

From Britt Forhman, founder of the Juicy Ladies Retreat. Shared with attribution.

I understand why so many moms don’t want sex anymore. Whether I’m in online or IRL moms’ groups or talking to friends or clients, my world seems to be full of women that have lost their desire for or interest in sex.

Our hormones aren’t what they used to be.

We have children hanging all over us.

We are exhausted.

We have a million other things to do that seem way more important.

On top of all of that, add a partner that is desperately hungry for sexy time and it just starts to feel like ONE.MORE.THING.TO.DO. Ugh.

But! The thing is that sex can be nourishing! It can be relaxing, energizing, enlivening, inspiring and re-connecting. So how do we find our way back?

Yes, we all know that taking time and space away from kids, and even partners, to recharge can be a big help to our libidos. But one thing that most advice is missing is this…

Find out what you do want, in this body, at this time in your life. Taking time to explore your own body, to feel your own yoni, your whole body, will help you sense into what kind of pleasure you actually desire. From that connection with yourself, you can then tell your partner what’s a YES for you, and what needs to wait for later.

If your partner is a man, they might think that what was working for you two before the baby is what they could expect now if you’d just come around. But most of us never even knew what we really wanted in the first place, and just went along with what they offered, liked or expected of us. We were trained to let them lead, or to like what they like. Combine motherhood with middle age and you get women that are no longer willing to put up with sex that doesn’t feel amazing.

My clients that take the time to discover their own bodies and desires, and then find ways to communicate that with their partners, are the ones that go on to having fulfilling, nourishing and pleasurable sex lives.

You deserve sex that you love and pleasure that heals, enlivens and inspires! And I know it’s possible for you, as I have seen tons of women (re)learn to light themselves up and get what they really want!

— Britt Forhman, founder of the Juicy Ladies Retreat
