Team Tools

Practices our teams use to get the most from every meeting.

Team Meeting Best Practices

Guiding Principles

  1. Start and end on time.
  2. Emotional depth over storytelling. Focus on what matters beneath the events of your life.
  3. Listening over fixing. Resist the urge to rescue, advise, or solve.
  4. Curiosity over certainty. Offer observations and possibilities rather than claiming to know another man's truth.
  5. Courage over comfort. Be willing to share a meaningful challenge when it may support another man's growth.
  6. Receiving over defending. Let another man's perspective land before deciding what to do with it.

1. Clear to Be Here

  • Keep it brief and focused on what is emotionally present.
  • Allow the man to express what he needs to express without turning it into a full check-in.
  • Listen without trying to resolve the issue.
  • Help create the conditions for him to become present to himself and the group.

2. Check-In: “Share What Matters” (3 - 5 min)

  • Emotional depth: Share what is creating joy, fear, anger, sadness, anxiety, uncertainty, or other significant feelings.
  • Personal ownership: Focus on your own experience, reactions, beliefs, and behavior rather than simply describing what other people did.
  • Meaning over chronology: A laundry list of activities and events is less useful than exploring the one or two experiences that are truly affecting you.
  • Authenticity: Be willing to share what is difficult, uncomfortable, unresolved, or vulnerable.

The role of the men listening

  • Be present to the man and his narrative.
  • Notice what you feel in response to what he shares.
  • Pay attention to what resonates, what creates tension, and what makes you curious.
  • Notice possible assumptions, blind spots, or alternative ways of understanding his experience.
  • Resist the urge to interrupt, ask questions, offer advice, or solve his problem.

3. Reverse Check-In: “What Came Up in You” (~ 1min)

  • Speak from your own experience. Share what you noticed, felt, or recognized while listening. Own your interpretation rather than presenting it as an objective truth.
  • Offer curiosity rather than certainty. Instead of telling a man what is wrong with his thinking, offer a possibility for him to consider.
  • Challenge with compassion. If you see a possible blind spot, inconsistency, or limiting belief, have the courage to share it. Do so in service of the man's growth, not to demonstrate that you are right.
  • Offer possibilities, not prescriptions. The goal is to expand awareness, not tell another man what to do.
  • Keep it concise. Share the insight that feels most valuable rather than delivering a lengthy analysis.
  • Allow positive feedback, but go deeper. Appreciation and acknowledgment have their place. However, do not stop at praise when you sense an opportunity to offer a meaningful reflection or challenge. Try not to say what you think he wants to hear.
  • The power of an unanswered question. When you offer this kind of question, you are not asking the man to answer you. You are placing a possibility in front of him for his own consideration.

4. Receiving the Reverse Check-In: Listen and Say Thank You

  • Listen to understand, not to rebut.
  • Remain open to perspectives that differ from your own.
  • Resist the impulse to explain why your actions made sense or why another man's interpretation is incorrect.
  • If someone offers a question, consider it without feeling compelled to answer.
  • Notice any emotional reaction or defensiveness that arises. That reaction itself may be worth exploring later.
  • Allow the feedback to land in you. You do not have to agree with every observation to consider it.

At the end of each reverse check-in, the receiving man simply says: “Thank you.”
